# Interview Panel — Bar Raiser Bank

20 behavioural questions for the `bar-raiser` seat: a round in the style of Amazon's Leadership
Principles, as widely copied by other companies. Each question's `topic` is the principle it
tests. Descriptions are paraphrased from Amazon's own published page, not quoted at length; each
`evidence` field below is a short, exact excerpt from that page supporting the rubric, per this
skill's sourcing rule.

**Topics (the principles, and how many questions test each):** `customer-obsession` (2),
`ownership` (2), `invent-and-simplify` (2), `have-backbone-disagree-and-commit` (2),
`deliver-results` (2), `earn-trust` (2), `bias-for-action` (2), `dive-deep` (2),
`are-right-a-lot` (1), `learn-and-be-curious` (1), `hire-and-develop-the-best` (1),
`frugality` (1).

Every question below is grounded in Amazon's own Leadership Principles page (retrieved
2026-09-24) — each principle's own definition is the source for what a strong answer
demonstrates. What shapes the *format* of this round — heavy use of "tell me about a time",
probing for the candidate's own actions and data, the STAR structure — comes from Amazon's own
published interview-prep guidance; see `references/personas/bar-raiser.md` and the source list
at the bottom of this file. This bank does not claim anything about Amazon's internal Bar Raiser
process beyond what those public pages state.

### BR01 Pushing Back for the Customer
- seat: bar-raiser
- topic: customer-obsession
- type: behavioural
- level: both
- question: Tell me about a time you pushed back on your own team or your leadership because it was the wrong call for the customer.
- strong answer contains:
  - Names a specific customer impact, not a general principle about caring about customers
  - Isolates what *I* said and did to push back, distinct from what the team eventually decided
  - Gives a concrete, checkable result — what changed, and whether the customer outcome actually improved
  - Is honest if the pushback didn't fully succeed, rather than implying it always wins
- red flags:
  - "We" throughout — the candidate's own specific action never isolated
  - No real result named, only that the pushback "was heard"
- follow-ups:
  - What did *you* specifically say, not what did the team eventually decide?
  - What would you do differently if you had that conversation again today?
- source: https://www.amazon.jobs/content/en/our-workplace/leadership-principles (retrieved 2026-09-24)
- source-type: answer
- evidence: "Leaders start with the customer and work backwards."

### BR02 Wanted vs Needed
- seat: bar-raiser
- topic: customer-obsession
- type: behavioural
- level: principal
- question: Tell me about a time you had to choose between what a customer said they wanted and what you knew they actually needed.
- strong answer contains:
  - States plainly what the customer asked for and what the candidate believed they actually needed, and why those differed
  - Describes the candidate's own reasoning and evidence for that belief, not just a hunch
  - Names the actual decision made and the data behind it, including if it turned out to be wrong
  - Reports the measurable outcome, not just that the decision was made
- red flags:
  - Can't articulate why the stated want and the real need were different
  - No data or evidence behind the candidate's own judgment call, just an opinion
- follow-ups:
  - What data did *you* look at before deciding, specifically?
  - What was the actual result, and what would you do differently now?
- source: none (common practice)
- source-type: none

### BR03 Owning What Wasn't Yours
- seat: bar-raiser
- topic: ownership
- type: behavioural
- level: both
- question: Tell me about a time you took on a problem that wasn't officially your job, and no one asked you to fix it.
- strong answer contains:
  - Names the specific problem and why it mattered beyond the candidate's own team or role
  - Describes exactly what *I* did, not what "the team" or "we" eventually did
  - States the actual outcome, with a number or a concrete change where possible
  - Shows this cost the candidate something — time, credit risk, political capital — not a costless nice-to-have
- red flags:
  - The story is really about someone assigning the work to them
  - No specific personal action isolated from the group's
- follow-ups:
  - What exactly did *you* do, separate from anyone else involved?
  - What was the measurable result, and what would you do differently next time?
- source: https://www.amazon.jobs/content/en/our-workplace/leadership-principles (retrieved 2026-09-24)
- source-type: answer
- evidence: "They act on behalf of the entire company, beyond just their own team."

### BR04 Long-Term Value Over Short-Term Win
- seat: bar-raiser
- topic: ownership
- type: behavioural
- level: principal
- question: Tell me about a decision you made that traded a short-term win for a longer-term outcome, even though it cost you something in the short term.
- strong answer contains:
  - Names the concrete short-term cost the candidate personally accepted — a missed metric, a harder conversation, delayed credit
  - Describes the reasoning for prioritizing the long-term outcome, backed by something more than instinct
  - Reports whether the long-term bet actually paid off, with a real result, not just that it was "the right call"
  - Is candid if the long-term outcome didn't fully materialize
- red flags:
  - No real short-term cost named — the trade-off was actually painless
  - The long-term payoff is asserted with no way to check it
- follow-ups:
  - What specifically did it cost *you*, personally, in the short term?
  - Looking back, would you make the same call, and what would you do differently?
- source: https://www.amazon.jobs/content/en/our-workplace/leadership-principles (retrieved 2026-09-24)
- source-type: answer
- evidence: "They think long term and don’t sacrifice long-term value for short-term results."

### BR05 Simplifying What Had Become Complex
- seat: bar-raiser
- topic: invent-and-simplify
- type: behavioural
- level: both
- question: Tell me about a time you simplified something that had become needlessly complex — a system, a process, an architecture.
- strong answer contains:
  - Names the specific complexity and what it was actually costing — time, incidents, onboarding difficulty
  - Isolates the candidate's own proposal and the work *they* did to simplify it, not a team-wide effort described collectively
  - States a measurable before/after — fewer steps, fewer components, faster time to do the thing
  - Names what was deliberately removed, not just what was added
- red flags:
  - "We simplified it" with the candidate's own specific contribution never named
  - No measurable before/after, just a claim that things got better
- follow-ups:
  - What exactly did *you* propose or build, separate from the rest of the team?
  - What would you simplify further if you did it again today?
- source: https://www.amazon.jobs/content/en/our-workplace/leadership-principles (retrieved 2026-09-24)
- source-type: answer
- evidence: "Leaders expect and require innovation and invention from their teams and always find ways to simplify."

### BR06 An Idea Met With Skepticism
- seat: bar-raiser
- topic: invent-and-simplify
- type: behavioural
- level: principal
- question: Tell me about a time you proposed an idea that was met with skepticism because it hadn't been done before, here or anywhere.
- strong answer contains:
  - States the specific idea and the specific skepticism it faced, not a vague "people doubted me"
  - Describes what *I* did to build the case — a prototype, data, a small pilot — not just persistence
  - Reports the actual outcome, including if the idea was later abandoned or changed significantly
  - Shows self-awareness about what the skeptics got right, if anything
- red flags:
  - Skepticism is framed as simply being proven wrong, with no credit to any valid concern raised
  - No concrete evidence-building step named, just conviction
- follow-ups:
  - What did *you* specifically build or show to make the case, not just argue for it?
  - What would you do differently in how you built that case?
- source: https://www.amazon.jobs/content/en/our-workplace/leadership-principles (retrieved 2026-09-24)
- source-type: answer
- evidence: "As we do new things, we accept that we may be misunderstood for long periods of time."

### BR07 Losing the Argument
- seat: bar-raiser
- topic: have-backbone-disagree-and-commit
- type: behavioural
- level: both
- question: Tell me about a time you disagreed with a decision, made your case, and still lost the argument. What did you do next?
- strong answer contains:
  - States the specific disagreement and the case the candidate personally made, with reasoning or evidence, not just a stated preference
  - Describes what *I* did after losing — genuinely committed, or quietly worked around the decision
  - Reports the actual outcome of the decision that was made, including if it turned out to be right
  - Reflects honestly on whether commitment was genuine or performative
- red flags:
  - Claims to always win the argument, or reframes a loss as a hidden win
  - "Committing" turns out to mean quietly undermining the decision afterward
- follow-ups:
  - What did *you* specifically do differently in your own work after the decision went against you?
  - Looking back, was the decision that won actually right, and what would you do differently in how you disagreed?
- source: https://www.amazon.jobs/content/en/our-workplace/leadership-principles (retrieved 2026-09-24)
- source-type: answer
- evidence: "Leaders are obligated to respectfully challenge decisions when they disagree, even when doing so is uncomfortable or exhausting."

### BR08 Committing to a Decision You Didn't Choose
- seat: bar-raiser
- topic: have-backbone-disagree-and-commit
- type: behavioural
- level: principal
- question: Tell me about a time you had to commit fully to a decision you didn't originally agree with.
- strong answer contains:
  - Names the specific decision and exactly why the candidate personally disagreed with it
  - Describes concrete actions the candidate took to genuinely execute it well, not just comply
  - States what actually happened once committed — including if their original objection turned out to matter
  - Distinguishes commitment from silent resentment or later undermining
- red flags:
  - "Committing" is described but the candidate's actions afterward still show resistance
  - No concrete result of the decision reported, only that they "went along with it"
- follow-ups:
  - What specifically did *you* do to execute it well, not just avoid blocking it?
  - What would you do differently in how you raised your original disagreement?
- source: https://www.amazon.jobs/content/en/our-workplace/leadership-principles (retrieved 2026-09-24)
- source-type: answer
- evidence: "They do not compromise for the sake of social cohesion. Once a decision is determined, they commit wholly."

### BR09 Delivering Through a Setback
- seat: bar-raiser
- topic: deliver-results
- type: behavioural
- level: both
- question: Tell me about a time you delivered a result despite a serious setback partway through.
- strong answer contains:
  - Names the specific setback and its real impact on the work, not a minor inconvenience dressed up
  - Describes what *I* specifically did in response, separate from what the team did collectively
  - States the actual, measurable result delivered, and how it compared to the original target
  - Is honest about any cost the recovery took — scope cut, quality trade-off, personal hours
- red flags:
  - The "setback" is trivial or invented after the fact to make the story more dramatic
  - No measurable result — "we got there in the end" with nothing to check
- follow-ups:
  - What did *you* personally do differently once the setback hit?
  - What would you do differently if you saw that setback coming again?
- source: https://www.amazon.jobs/content/en/our-workplace/leadership-principles (retrieved 2026-09-24)
- source-type: answer
- evidence: "Despite setbacks, they rise to the occasion and never settle."

### BR10 The Most Ambitious Deadline
- seat: bar-raiser
- topic: deliver-results
- type: behavioural
- level: principal
- question: Tell me about the most ambitious deadline you've hit. What did it actually take?
- strong answer contains:
  - Names the specific deadline and why it was genuinely ambitious, not just tight
  - Isolates what *I* did to hit it, distinct from the rest of the team's contribution
  - Names the real inputs it took — hours, trade-offs, what was deprioritized — not a frictionless success story
  - States the actual quality of what was delivered, not just that the date was hit
- red flags:
  - Hitting the date is treated as success regardless of what was cut to get there
  - No personal contribution isolated from the team's
- follow-ups:
  - What specifically did *you* do that the deadline wouldn't have been hit without?
  - What would you do differently if you had that timeline again?
- source: https://www.amazon.jobs/content/en/our-workplace/leadership-principles (retrieved 2026-09-24)
- source-type: answer
- evidence: "Leaders focus on the key inputs for their business and deliver them with the right quality and in a timely fashion."

### BR11 Telling Someone What They Didn't Want to Hear
- seat: bar-raiser
- topic: earn-trust
- type: behavioural
- level: both
- question: Tell me about a time you had to tell a team, a peer, or leadership something they didn't want to hear.
- strong answer contains:
  - States plainly what the hard truth was and who needed to hear it
  - Describes specifically how *I* delivered it — candidly, with evidence — not just that it was said
  - Reports the actual reaction and what happened afterward, including if it damaged the relationship short term
  - Shows the candidate benchmarked their own judgment against evidence, not just asserted an opinion
- red flags:
  - The "hard truth" was actually easy or already agreed by everyone
  - No account of the actual reaction or aftermath, only that it was said
- follow-ups:
  - What exactly did *you* say, in your own words, not a summary of the message?
  - What would you do differently in how you delivered it?
- source: https://www.amazon.jobs/content/en/our-workplace/leadership-principles (retrieved 2026-09-24)
- source-type: answer
- evidence: "Leaders listen attentively, speak candidly, and treat others respectfully."

### BR12 Owning a Mistake First
- seat: bar-raiser
- topic: earn-trust
- type: behavioural
- level: lead
- question: Tell me about a time you were candid about your own mistake before anyone else had noticed it.
- strong answer contains:
  - Names the specific mistake and its real consequence, not a minor slip inflated for the story
  - Describes exactly what *I* did to surface it — who was told, how soon, how directly
  - States what actually happened as a result, including any real cost to the candidate
  - Shows what changed afterward in how they work, not just an apology
- red flags:
  - The "mistake" is humble-bragging in disguise, with no real negative consequence
  - No account of what changed afterward
- follow-ups:
  - What exactly did *you* say, and to whom, and how soon after you noticed?
  - What would you do differently to have caught it sooner?
- source: https://www.amazon.jobs/content/en/our-workplace/leadership-principles (retrieved 2026-09-24)
- source-type: answer
- evidence: "They are vocally self-critical, even when doing so is awkward or embarrassing."

### BR13 Deciding Without Certainty
- seat: bar-raiser
- topic: bias-for-action
- type: behavioural
- level: both
- question: Tell me about a time you made a call with incomplete information because waiting for certainty would have cost too much.
- strong answer contains:
  - States specifically what information was missing and why waiting for it had a real cost
  - Describes what *I* personally decided and the reasoning used to decide anyway
  - Reports the actual outcome, including if the decision turned out to be wrong
  - Shows the decision was a calculated risk, not a guess with no reasoning behind it
- red flags:
  - The "incomplete information" framing is invented — the decision was actually well-supported already
  - No real reasoning behind the call, just "I went with my gut"
- follow-ups:
  - What specifically did *you* decide, and what evidence did you have at that moment?
  - What would you do differently, knowing what you know now?
- source: https://www.amazon.jobs/content/en/our-workplace/leadership-principles (retrieved 2026-09-24)
- source-type: answer
- evidence: "Many decisions and actions are reversible and do not need extensive study."

### BR14 Making a Decision Reversible
- seat: bar-raiser
- topic: bias-for-action
- type: behavioural
- level: principal
- question: Tell me about a decision you deliberately made reversible, so you could move fast without excessive risk.
- strong answer contains:
  - Names the specific decision and the specific mechanism that made it reversible — a flag, a staged rollout, a fallback
  - Describes what *I* specifically built or chose to create that reversibility, not a general design philosophy
  - States whether it actually had to be reversed, and what happened when it was (or wasn't)
  - Distinguishes this from a decision that was reckless because reversal was never actually possible
- red flags:
  - "Reversible" turns out to mean nothing concrete — no real rollback mechanism existed
  - No account of whether reversal was ever tested or needed
- follow-ups:
  - What exactly did *you* build to make it reversible, specifically?
  - What would you do differently in how you designed the reversibility?
- source: https://www.amazon.jobs/content/en/our-workplace/leadership-principles (retrieved 2026-09-24)
- source-type: answer
- evidence: "We value calculated risk taking."

### BR15 When the Dashboard Was Wrong
- seat: bar-raiser
- topic: dive-deep
- type: behavioural
- level: both
- question: Tell me about a time a dashboard or a status report said one thing and you didn't believe it — what did you do?
- strong answer contains:

  - Describes exactly what *I* did to go verify it — which system, which data, which person

### BR16 Hands-On Below Your Day-to-Day
- seat: bar-raiser
- topic: dive-deep
- type: behavioural
- level: principal
- question: Tell me about the last time you were hands-on in the details of a system several levels below your day-to-day scope.
- strong answer contains:
  - Names the specific system and level of detail the candidate got into personally
  - Explains why it mattered to go that deep rather than delegate or trust a summary
  - Describes exactly what *I* found or did at that level, not what "the team investigated"
  - States what changed as a result of going that deep
- red flags:
  - The "hands-on" story is actually secondhand — someone else did the digging and reported up
  - No concrete finding or change resulted from the deep dive
- follow-ups:
  - What specifically did *you* personally look at or do, at that level of detail?
  - What would you do differently about how often you go that deep?

### BR17 Changing Your Mind
- seat: bar-raiser
- topic: are-right-a-lot
- type: behavioural
- level: principal
- question: Tell me about a time you changed your mind on a significant technical position after hearing someone else's view.
- strong answer contains:
  - States the candidate's original position and why they held it, specifically
  - Describes what *I* heard or saw that actually changed the reasoning, not just social pressure to conform
  - Reports what the candidate did once their mind changed — reversed a decision, said so publicly
  - Shows this wasn't costless — a real decision or public position had to be walked back
- red flags:
  - The "change of mind" was actually trivial or cost nothing to admit
  - No account of what specifically changed the candidate's reasoning
- follow-ups:
  - What specifically did the other person say or show that changed *your* mind?
  - What would you do differently in how you held your original position?

- source-type: answer
- evidence: "They seek diverse perspectives and work to disconfirm their beliefs."

### BR18 Learning Outside the Job Description
- seat: bar-raiser
- topic: learn-and-be-curious
- type: behavioural
- level: lead
- question: Tell me about something you deliberately went and learned, outside of what your role required, that later mattered.
- strong answer contains:
  - Names the specific thing learned and why the candidate chose to pursue it, unprompted by the role
  - Describes what *I* actually did to learn it — a course, a side project, hands-on practice — not a passive claim
  - States concretely how it later mattered — a decision it improved, a problem it let them solve
  - Distinguishes genuine curiosity from something learned only because it was required later
- red flags:
  - The "learning" was actually mandated training, not self-directed curiosity
  - No concrete later use named — the learning is asserted as valuable with no evidence
- follow-ups:
  - What specifically did *you* do to learn it, and how much time did it take?
  - What are you deliberately learning right now, outside your current role?
- source: https://www.amazon.jobs/content/en/our-workplace/leadership-principles (retrieved 2026-09-24)
- source-type: answer
- evidence: "Leaders are never done learning and always seek to improve themselves."

### BR19 Developing Someone Past You
- seat: bar-raiser
- topic: hire-and-develop-the-best
- type: behavioural
- level: principal
- question: Tell me about someone you developed who ended up more capable than you expected, or who you helped get promoted past a level you had to fight for.
- strong answer contains:
  - Names the specific person's starting point and what the candidate specifically did to develop them — coaching, stretch assignments, sponsorship
  - Isolates what *I* did, not "the team supported them" or "they were naturally talented"
  - States the concrete outcome — a promotion, a specific capability gained — with a real marker, not a vague impression
  - Shows the candidate's own reaction to being outgrown or matched, rather than glossing over it
- red flags:
  - Credits the person's growth entirely to their own talent, with no candidate action named
  - No concrete outcome — a title, a scope change — just a general sense of growth
- follow-ups:
  - What specifically did *you* do to develop them, distinct from what they did on their own?
  - What would you do differently in how you coached them?
- source: https://www.amazon.jobs/content/en/our-workplace/leadership-principles (retrieved 2026-09-24)
- source-type: answer
- evidence: "Leaders develop leaders and take seriously their role in coaching others."

### BR20 Delivering With Less
- seat: bar-raiser
- topic: frugality
- type: behavioural
- level: lead
- question: Tell me about a time you delivered a result with deliberately fewer resources than you were offered.
- strong answer contains:
  - Names the specific resources declined or not requested — headcount, budget, infrastructure — and why
  - Describes what *I* did instead to hit the goal with the constraint, not a team-wide belt-tightening story
  - States the actual result delivered and how it compared to what more resources would plausibly have bought
  - Shows this was a deliberate choice, not resource scarcity imposed on the candidate with no alternative
- red flags:
  - The constraint was imposed, not chosen — reframed after the fact as a virtue
  - No real result to show for the constrained approach, just that it was "scrappy"
- follow-ups:
  - What specifically did *you* choose not to ask for, and why?
  - What would you do differently if you had the resources you turned down?
- source: https://www.amazon.jobs/content/en/our-workplace/leadership-principles (retrieved 2026-09-24)
- source-type: answer
- evidence: "Constraints breed resourcefulness, self-sufficiency, and invention."

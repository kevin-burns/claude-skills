# Elicitation question bank

Organised by what each question is trying to dislodge. Pick three to five per
batch, matched to what the CV is actually missing — working through this list
in order produces a survey, not a conversation.

## Why oblique questions work

Direct questions ask people to self-assess, and they already did that when they
wrote the CV. "What were your key achievements?" returns the bullets already on
the page, in slightly different words.

Oblique questions ask about something else — difficulty, causation, other
people, what didn't happen — and the achievement falls out as a by-product.
The person isn't being asked to judge themselves, so the modesty filter that
suppressed the material in the first place doesn't engage.

## Dislodging the invisible specialism

The thing colleagues always come to them for, which never had a title.

- What do people come to you for, that isn't in your job description?
- What would your team lead say you were relied on for?
- If you went on leave for a month, what would break or stall?
- What do you get asked to review or sanity-check before it ships?
- Who did you teach, mentor or unblock, and what changed for them?

## Dislodging difficulty and judgement

Difficulty is where the interesting engineering and the interesting decisions
live. It also produces stories that survive interviews.

- What was the hardest part of that, and why was it hard?
- What did you try first that didn't work?
- What was the decision you were least sure about?
- What would you do differently if you started it again?
- What did you have to talk someone out of?
- What constraint made this harder than it should have been?

## Dislodging reasoning behind tool and approach choices

Aim these at anyone whose CV leans on a technology list, a methodology, or a
stack of certifications. The list itself is nearly worthless as evidence —
anyone can name the tools. What separates a senior practitioner from someone who
attended the course is why they chose one thing over another and what it cost
them, and that never appears unless it is asked for.

- Why that tool, rather than the obvious alternative? What was the alternative?
- What did that choice cost you — what got harder because of it?
- Would you choose the same thing again today? What changed your mind?
- What did you have to learn to make that work?
- What did that project teach you that you now apply everywhere?
- Where did the approach not work, and how did you find out?
- What did you build with it, and who used it?

These also produce interview answers directly, since this is roughly the
sequence a technical interviewer works through.

## Dislodging causation and counterfactual

Separates what they *did* from what merely happened around them — which is
exactly the distinction a sceptical reader is trying to make.

- Why were *you* the person asked to do it?
- What would have happened if you hadn't been there?
- Would it have shipped without you? Would it have shipped differently?
- What was in place before, and who set it up?
- Was this your idea, or were you handed it?

## Dislodging the unclaimed win

Material people leave out because it felt easy, incidental, or not "real work".

- What's the thing you're quietly proud of that isn't on here?
- What's on here that you found easy, that other people seem to find hard?
- Is there anything you built that's still running or still in use?
- Did anything you made get adopted by people you didn't build it for?
- What went badly wrong, and what did you change afterwards?

That last one is worth asking despite feeling risky. Recovery and correction are
strong evidence of judgement, and people almost never volunteer them.

## Follow-up probes

Once something surfaces, these turn a vague claim into usable evidence. Ask them
as natural curiosity, not as an audit — the goal is a number the person can
defend, not a number that sounds impressive.

**Scale** — how many, how big, how long, how much. Accounts, users, teams,
requests, euros, terabytes, regions, engineers. If the person doesn't know, ask
what order of magnitude, and record it as approximate.

**Baseline** — compared to what? "Reduced costs by 35%" is meaningless without
the starting point, and a reader who cares will assume the worst. What was it
before, over what period, and how was it measured?

**Attribution** — who else was involved, and what part was theirs? Overclaiming
gets found out in interviews. "I did X within a team of six that delivered Y" is
stronger than an ambiguous "led", because it survives scrutiny.

**Durability** — is it still in use? Did it survive them leaving? Did anyone
else adopt it? Durability is the difference between a project and an
achievement, and it is the probe people most often haven't thought about.

**Verification** — how do they know? A dashboard they can still open, a figure a
former colleague would confirm, a number from a report. This determines whether
the entry is tagged confirmed, approximate or unverified, and it matters because
an interviewer may ask exactly this.

## Handling thin answers

When an answer comes back short or flat, the usual cause is that the question
was too abstract or the person doesn't think the material counts.

- Get specific and concrete: ask about one particular instance, not the general
  pattern. "Tell me about the worst incident" beats "tell me about reliability."
- Ask about the surrounding narrative rather than the achievement: who was
  unhappy before, who was happy after, what conversation triggered the work.
- Try the counterfactual: what the absence would have looked like is often
  easier to describe than the contribution.
- If it stays thin after two attempts, move on and note it in the coverage
  table. It may open up in a later session, and pushing costs more goodwill than
  the material is worth.

## Sensitive territory

Gaps, exits, health, caring responsibilities, discrimination. These matter
because a screener will flag them, but the way they're asked determines whether
the person keeps talking at all.

Ask once, neutrally, with an easy exit: "There's a gap between X and Y — is
that something you want on the page, or would you rather we work around it?"
Accept a decline immediately and without follow-up, and move to solving it as a
positioning problem. There is almost always a framing that neither lies nor
invites the question.
